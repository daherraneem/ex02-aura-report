# Committee review — Professor of Marketing Communications

Scope: the full report (abstract, chapters 1–8, references.bib) as source (`chapters/*.tex`) and compiled `main.pdf` (body pp. 1–37), judged on content, theory use, and the logic of the argument chain problem → literature → market → alternative → plan → risks. Criteria checked against the lecturer's booklet (Part A, chapters 4, 5, 7 and 9) and the L04 summary (section 9); no text from them is quoted here.

1. **MP-01 · critical** — **Problem:** The knowledge gap is only a gap in combinations ("each tool was studied alone, none together"). The launch design also cannot close it. The gap sentence says the plan "is built to measure exactly this combination" of food name + tasting + micro-influencers on awareness and first sales. But every campus gets the full bundle at once, with no control campus, no staggered rollout, no holdout group and no factorial split. So no effect can be traced to any one element or to their interaction. A single uncontrolled launch confounds all four tools with seasonality, the creators' reach and paid retargeting. The matrix row "Project — tested together" (tab:lit-matrix) is a claim the design cannot deliver. Also, "nobody combined A+B+C+D" is true of almost any bundle, and the chapter never argues why the interaction should matter (synergy or interference). **Where:** `chapters/03-literature.tex:220-223`, `:341-355` (keymessage), `:215`; `chapters/08-summary.tex:12-14`; abstract `00-abstract.tex:19-21`; PDF body pp. 12, 15. **Concrete fix:** Either reframe the gap as an applied/transfer question (do lab-proven mechanisms hold in a real Israeli campus launch of a cosmetic?), or add a quasi-experimental layer to the plan. For example: matched pilot campuses vs. non-pilot campuses with pre/post awareness surveys at both (difference-in-differences); or, inside the bars, a tasting arm vs. a shade-only arm on alternating hours with separate coupon codes. Then state the hypothesis about the interaction explicitly.

2. **MP-02 · critical** — **Problem:** The flagship model is misapplied in measurement. Bawa & Shoemaker separate expansion, acceleration and cannibalization by comparing sampled and non-sampled households in field experiments with purchase-panel data. The report replaces that with one self-report question at the stall ("did you plan to buy?"). For a brand with zero awareness and no sales channel, almost no one can truthfully have "planned to buy" AURA. So C ≈ 0 by construction, and E is inflated by social-desirability bias. G5 (≥10% of sample recipients buy with the stall code) is labelled as the measure of E (`:311-313`), but it counts every coupon purchase, cannibalized ones included. A is redefined as "a second purchase within 30 days". That is not the paper's construct (purchases shifted in time), and it is implausible for a 10 ml tint. The report never defines what a "sample" is: an on-lip tester at the stall, or a take-home unit like in the paper. Without that, the 3,000 "samples" (G3) are not comparable to the model at all. As a result, the "high replicability" rating in tab:flagship-criteria, which decided the flagship choice, is overstated. **Where:** `chapters/03-literature.tex:249`, `:276-290`, `:308-339` (tab:flagship-measures); `chapters/02-problem.tex:249-251`; PDF body pp. 13–14. **Concrete fix:** Define the sample unit (take-home mini vs. in-stall trial). Estimate expansion against a comparison group: purchase rate of matched students at non-pilot campuses, or of stall visitors who did not receive a sample. Drop or re-specify A for the 6-month window. Rename G5 "trial-to-purchase conversion", not "expansion". Re-score replicability honestly, or justify the choice on other grounds.

3. **MP-03 · critical** — **Problem:** The sales target G4 (₪255K), which drives the abstract, the problem statement, the guiding question and the channel split, is not justified and is internally inconsistent. (a) SAM assumes 70% "digital-cosmetic adoption", taken from social-media usage rates. That measures platform use, not lip-product buying. (b) 2.5 units per year per buyer has no source. (c) The SOM of 1.5–2.5% "for a new D2C brand" has no citation. (d) 2,960 customers ≈ 7,400 units means 2.5 units per customer: each SOM customer buys her whole assumed annual tint consumption from AURA. That is penetration and share-of-wallet treated as one thing. (e) ₪255K in 3 selling months is 50% of the year-1 SOM (₪511K), achieved from zero awareness. That is the opposite of "conservative". (f) The realism check "<0.04% of the Israeli cosmetics market" compares against the entire cosmetics TAM, which is irrelevant. Against the SAM, it is ~1% of a year's category spend in one quarter. (g) 3,700 units × ₪69 ≈ ₪255K implies all single units at list price, which contradicts the ₪115 D2C AOV with ₪299 packs in tab:channel-architecture. "Equivalent units" is never defined. **Where:** `chapters/04-market.tex:78-95`, `:107-108`, `:122-125`; `chapters/02-problem.tex:195-197`, `:236-238`, `:256-257`; `chapters/05-alternatives.tex:279-302`; PDF body pp. 5–6, 18–19, 28. **Concrete fix:** Rebuild G4 bottom-up from a funnel: creator reach × view-through × aided awareness × consideration × trial × purchase × AOV, plus stall traffic × conversion. Each rate must come from a cited benchmark or be marked as an assumption, with a low/base/high case. Check realism against the SAM, not the TAM. Define units and AOV once and use them in every chapter.

4. **MP-04 · major** — **Problem:** The communication goals are invented and not tied to a funnel. The report admits the values are "planning targets of the team" (`02-problem.tex:236`). G1 (≥20% aided awareness among students at pilot campuses) has no baseline, no benchmark, no sampling frame, and no link to how many students the 3 campuses hold or how many the 3,000 samples reach. G2 (1,000,000 cumulative views) is not derived from 20–30 creators with 5–30K followers and the cited engagement rates. Views are a vanity metric with no reach/frequency logic. No metric covers unaided awareness, consideration, purchase intent, brand associations (does "AURA" = food-inspired, warm, affordable?), share of voice, earned media value or cost per acquisition per channel. The guiding question asks about awareness and sales, but the plan has no step between the two. **Where:** `chapters/02-problem.tex:206-208`, `:236-260` (tab:smart-goals), `:291` (REQ-09); `chapters/06-workplan.tex` (M5/M6 rows); PDF body pp. 5–6, 33. **Concrete fix:** Add an awareness-to-purchase funnel table (exposure → reach → aided/unaided awareness → consideration → trial → purchase → repeat/advocacy). Give each stage a KPI, a target with its justification, a data source and a measurement week. Make REQ-09 a proper tracking study: baseline + endline at pilot and control campuses, unaided before aided, and an image/association battery.

5. **MP-05 · major** — **Problem:** The naming theory is misapplied. Miller & Kahn's mechanism (Gricean inference) works because the name is *unexpected or ambiguous*, i.e. not informative about the colour, so the consumer looks for the reason. "Golden Honey Glaze", "Velvet Peach Nectar", "Silk Cocoa Glaze" are typical, descriptive food-colour names (honey = golden, cocoa = brown). In the paper's typicality/specificity terms they are closer to common descriptive names than to ambiguous ones. So the claim that they trigger "exactly the search for meaning" (`:99-103`) is not supported. The proposed tasting step also *resolves* the ambiguity at once ("tastes honey, then tries the shade"). By the paper's own logic, that removes the inference that creates the effect. Skorinko et al. support the "fancy name" route only. Lowrey & Shrum is cited, but the English names are never checked for sound symbolism, and the audience reads Hebrew (and Arabic). The synthesis then maps the naming principle to G1 (awareness), which cannot isolate any naming effect. **Where:** `chapters/03-literature.tex:80-103`, `:178`; PDF body pp. 9–10, 11. **Concrete fix:** Re-classify the five names by Miller & Kahn's categories and state which effect (if any) is expected. Either accept that the support comes from Skorinko only, or test unexpected names. Explain why tasting adds value even though it removes ambiguity (e.g., via a congruence argument with its own source). Add a naming check for Hebrew/Arabic speakers (pronunciation, transliteration, meaning). Replace G1 with a measure suited to names (e.g., an A/B test of names on the D2C product page).

6. **MP-06 · major** — **Problem:** The sensory-marketing evidence is stretched past what the papers show. The core mechanic, "taste the food, then try the matching shade", has no source behind it. Elder & Krishna show that multisensory *ad copy for a food* raises the *perceived taste of that food*. Nothing in it covers moving value from an eaten food to a non-edible cosmetic. Peck & Childers and Peck & Shu concern *touching the product itself by hand*. Tasting another product is not haptic input about the tint. In ch. 5, Krishna (2012) is cited for "smell and touch are decisive in building trust in a sensory product". That claim about trust is stronger than an integrative review's general statements support. The review also skips the streams that actually fit the concept: cross-modal congruence (scent/taste–product fit), experiential/event marketing, and pop-up retail. **Where:** `chapters/03-literature.tex:63-75`, `:176-177`; `chapters/05-alternatives.tex:50-52`, `:329-339`; PDF body pp. 9, 24, 29. **Concrete fix:** Limit each claim to the paper's scope ("food ads", "hand touch of the product"). Search for and add verified peer-reviewed work on sensory congruence and on experiential/event marketing (find it by web search, check the DOI, never cite from memory). State the tasting-to-tint link as an untested hypothesis and measure it (see MP-01). Rewrite the ch. 5 Krishna sentence to what the review states.

7. **MP-07 · major** — **Problem:** The influencer studies are partly misread, and the trade-offs are hidden. (a) In De Veirman et al., the loss of perceived uniqueness for a divergent product appears when it is promoted by influencers *with many followers*. The report drops that condition (`:111-112`) and then presents it as a general "warning against launches" (`:132-133`). Read correctly, it is evidence *for* the micro-influencer choice, and the chapter misses that. (b) Evans et al. find that the "Paid Ad" wording raises ad recognition. Per the article, ad recognition (with disclosure memory) relates to *less* favourable brand attitude and *lower* sharing intention. The report describes this neutrally and then prescribes an explicit "advertisement" label everywhere, without noting the cost to sharing. G2 and REQ-10 (UGC) depend on sharing. (c) Leung et al. measure effectiveness as engagement on their own platform data, not sales. The report does not say which platform or country the data come from. Its remedy for the negative effect of launch posts ("experience content, not announcement") is not something Leung et al. tested. **Where:** `chapters/03-literature.tex:108-136`, `:179-181`, `:211-213`; PDF body pp. 10, 12. **Concrete fix:** Restore the follower-count condition and use it to justify micro-creators. Report the full Evans et al. result and the legal-vs-persuasion tension, and add a mitigation (e.g., disclosure wording that is legally clear but native to the creator's voice, tested in a pilot). State Leung et al.'s dependent variable and data context, and present the "experience content" remedy as the team's assumption.

8. **MP-08 · major** — **Problem:** A plan whose engine is creator content and campus events has no message strategy. There is no positioning statement (target – frame of reference – point of difference – reason to believe), no single-minded proposition, no creative concept or campaign idea, no content pillars, no tone of voice, no creator brief, no language strategy (Hebrew/Arabic/English), and no sequencing (teaser → launch → sustain). The only message guidance in the whole report is "experience, not announcement" and "label as ad". The decision matrix's top criterion, "fit to persona and sensory insight", is scored without any defined message to be fitted. **Where:** absent across chapters 2, 5, 6. Closest points: `chapters/05-alternatives.tex:228-265` (architecture), `:318-339`; `chapters/02-problem.tex:287` (REQ-05 counts posts only); PDF body pp. 28–30. **Concrete fix:** Add a short "message and creative strategy" subsection (in ch. 5 after the architecture) with a message house: umbrella claim, 3 pillars, proof points tied to the literature principles in tab:lit-principles, mandatory and forbidden claims (regulatory), and a 1-paragraph creator brief. Add a requirement for message consistency (e.g., % of creator posts carrying the key message, coded weekly).

9. **MP-09 · major** — **Problem:** The differentiation argument contradicts itself and is partly circular. Ch. 4 states that Rhode already uses food names, food scents and "appetite appeal" (`04-market.tex:39-43`), and the value index gives Rhode and AURA the same culinary-narrative score (2.4 = 2.4). Yet ch. 5 positions AURA against "Rhode's grey minimalism" (`05-alternatives.tex:332-333`), and tab:innovation-comparison claims "unique ownership in Israel" of culinary-beauty. So food inspiration is not the point of difference; local price and availability are. The value index is also self-scored by the authors. It ranks the not-yet-existing AURA highest (8.8), gives "social capital" 1.8 to a brand with zero awareness, and includes "accessibility / value per ml". That puts price on both axes of a price-vs-value map, which builds the "white space" into the measure. A gap on a price map is not evidence of demand: the report has no data on willingness to pay ₪69. **Where:** `chapters/04-market.tex:214-262` (tab:value-index, fig:price-positioning); `chapters/05-alternatives.tex:162-164`, `:383-384`; PDF body pp. 20–21, 29–30. **Concrete fix:** Either remove AURA from the scored set or score it from consumer data (a short perceptual-mapping survey of the target in the baseline wave of REQ-09). Take price out of the value index. Add a willingness-to-pay check (e.g., a Van Westendorp question in the baseline survey). Rewrite the point of difference as what is actually unique (local, tryable, mid-price), and make the ch. 4 and ch. 5 claims about Rhode match.

10. **MP-10 · major** — **Problem:** There is no Israeli or Gen Z-specific evidence for the target, and the segment definition does not fit Israel. The target is women 18–28, but the plan reaches only higher-education students. In Israel most women aged 18–20 are in mandatory military or national service, so students skew older and the 18–20 group is largely missed. The report never addresses this. The Gen Z discovery figures (TikTok 39%, Instagram 35%, the doubling of social commerce, low loyalty) come from a trade-press write-up of an agency survey (`meintjes2025genz`). The report does not state the survey's country, so it is not Israeli evidence, yet it drives the "visibility" gap. The DataReportal figures cover all adults, not 18–28. Arab students, a substantial share at several pilot-candidate campuses including Haifa, are not mentioned in channel or language terms. **Where:** `chapters/01-intro.tex:68-76`, `:86-90`; `chapters/02-problem.tex:182-184`; `chapters/04-market.tex:67-71`; references.bib `meintjes2025genz`; PDF body pp. 1–2, 4. **Concrete fix:** Either redefine the target as "female students (≈20–28)" and explain the gap for 18–20, or add a non-campus route for them. Find verified Israeli data on age distribution of students and on social-media use by age (e.g., CBS), or state clearly that the discovery figures are non-Israeli. Add channel-discovery questions to the REQ-09 baseline to test the assumption locally. Address the language mix of the campus audience.

11. **MP-11 · major** — **Problem:** The decision matrix that selects Alternative C rests on scores with no anchors and no evidence, and some scores contradict the descriptions. C gets 9 on virality/awareness, above A's 8, although A spends 100% on digital and C only 45%. C gets 9 on conversion because of marketplace "trust and next-day delivery", although marketplace onboarding is not secured (it is a month-1–2 task). C gets 9 on sensory fit, equal to B, although B runs four campuses with café partnerships and C runs 3 events × 1,000 samples, i.e. ~1.4% of the 211.6K students. The sensitivity analysis varies only weights, never scores. Yet a one-point change on C's virality or conversion score would close most of the 0.70 gap to A. **Where:** `chapters/05-alternatives.tex:161-222` (tab:decision-matrix, fig:decision-matrix); PDF body pp. 26–27. **Concrete fix:** Add a scoring rubric (what earns 3/6/9 for each criterion), one line of evidence per cell, and a score-sensitivity test (±1 on each of C's 9s and A's 8s). Report the break-even point. Ideally have a second scorer and report agreement.

12. **MP-12 · major** — **Problem:** The risk register leaves out the communication and concept risks specific to a food-inspired cosmetic, and one mitigation contradicts the regulatory path. Missing risks: (a) food-imitation / ingestion confusion. Cosmetics that look or smell like food are a known regulatory concern (e.g., EU Council Directive 87/357/EEC on products that appear to be foodstuffs), and the tasting bar plus "glaze/nectar" naming increases it. (b) Food-safety, allergen and hygiene exposure at the stalls (honey, cocoa, berries; shared lip testers). (c) Prohibited or medical-sounding claims in creator content, and negative UGC or backlash. Contradiction: the R5 mitigation runs student focus groups in months 1–2 "to refine texture, scent and wear", and the contingency "updates the formula in the second batch". But REQ-01 files the Ministry of Health notifications for all 5 shades in month 1. A change of formula after notification is likely to need re-notification on the critical path. The residual scores (e.g., 16 → 6) are asserted without any reasoning. **Where:** `chapters/07-risks.tex:36-42`, `:94-97`, `:102-106`; `chapters/06-workplan.tex` (WP1/M1, critical path); `chapters/02-problem.tex:283`; PDF body pp. 35–36. **Concrete fix:** Add risks R8–R10 (food imitation/ingestion; stall food-safety and hygiene; claims/backlash), each with a verified regulatory source. Move formula testing before the M1 notification, or add re-notification time to the Gantt. Justify each residual P and I in one line.

13. **MP-13 · minor** — **Problem:** Weak or misattributed evidence carries load-bearing claims. The 75% / 33% sampling statistic is a stated-intention trade-press survey, and its commissioning party is not identified. Yet it is the main empirical support for the "trial" gap in ch. 1, ch. 2 and ch. 3. Ch. 1 says "after receiving a sample", ch. 2 says "after trying a sample", which is not the same claim. In ch. 7 the same source and Krishna (2012) are cited for R5 (negative reviews of texture, scent, wear), and neither supports that risk. The AI Sommelier rests on cross-industry McKinsey personalization averages (10–15% revenue lift) and a vendor case study (Octane AI). That cannot support a 3-question quiz for an unknown brand. **Where:** `chapters/01-intro.tex:112-115`; `chapters/02-problem.tex:185-187`; `chapters/03-literature.tex:149-152`; `chapters/07-risks.tex:40`; `chapters/05-alternatives.tex:351-359`; PDF body pp. 3, 5, 11, 31, 35. **Concrete fix:** Name the survey's commissioner and its country, and use the same wording everywhere. Remove the R5 citations or replace them with a source on review/WOM risk. Present the Sommelier uplift as an assumption to be tested (A/B on the D2C site), not as an expected effect.

14. **MP-14 · minor** — **Problem:** The literature chapter does not deliver what the roadmap promises, and its scope is dated for a TikTok launch. Ch. 1 says ch. 3 reviews "influencer marketing, social commerce and sensory marketing" (`01-intro.tex:126-127`). Ch. 3 contains no social-commerce paper. All 12 papers are from 2003–2022, none studies TikTok, short video, Gen Z or Israel, and none covers word-of-mouth seeding or UGC challenges. REQ-10 and the "UGC flywheel" depend on exactly those. The matrix gives the project row "tested" in every column, which repeats the overclaim in MP-01. **Where:** `chapters/01-intro.tex:125-130`; `chapters/03-literature.tex:21-31`, `:187-218`; PDF body pp. 3, 8, 12. **Concrete fix:** Either fix the roadmap sentence or add a verified, peer-reviewed social-commerce/seeding stream (search, DOI-check, cite) with its own critical reading and matrix column. Change the project row to "planned measurement" rather than "tested".
